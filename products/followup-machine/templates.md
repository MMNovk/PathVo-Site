# The Follow-Up Machine

**48 cold-outreach and follow-up email templates for freelancers and small agencies.**

Every template below has four parts: a **Send when** timing note, one or more **subject lines**, the **full email body** with `[BRACKETED]` slots to fill, and a short **why this works** note so you can adapt it without breaking it.

Two rules before you send anything:

1. **Fill every bracket with something specific.** A half-personalized template reads worse than no email at all. If you can't fill `[SPECIFIC THING YOU NOTICED]`, don't send that template — pick another.
2. **One email, one ask.** Every template here ends with a single, small, answerable question. Keep it that way when you edit.

---

## The Master Cadence Map

How the sequences in this library chain together:

| Situation | Sequence | Spacing |
|---|---|---|
| Cold prospect | 1 opener (Section A) + follow-ups 10→13 (Section B) | Day 0 → 3 → 7 → 14 → 28 |
| Proposal sent | Templates 16→21 (Section C) | Day 2 → 6 → 11 → 16 → 21 → 30 (use 3–4 of the 6, not all) |
| Client went quiet mid-conversation | Templates 22→26 (Section D) | 1 week after silence, then weekly ×2, then close |
| Past client, project done | Section E, one touch per quarter | Rotate templates so it never repeats |
| Invoice due | Templates 32→36 (Section F) | Day −3 → +2 → +9 → +18 |
| Project just wrapped | Section G within 72 hours of the win | Strike while they're happy |
| Nothing has worked | Section H | One breakup email, then stop |

## Section A — Cold Outreach: First-Touch Openers

Nine openers in three families. Pick the one that matches what you actually have: a shared contact, a genuine observation, or directly relevant work.

### A. The Referral Openers

Use when someone — a client, a friend, a community — connects you, even loosely. Warmest cold email there is.

---

### 1. The Straight Name-Drop

**Send when:** A mutual contact explicitly said "you should talk to them" or agreed to let you use their name.

**Subject:** [MUTUAL CONTACT] suggested I reach out
**Alt subject:** [MUTUAL CONTACT] pointed me your way

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

[MUTUAL CONTACT] mentioned you're looking for help with [PROJECT/PROBLEM AREA] and suggested I get in touch.

I'm a [YOUR ROLE] — I recently did [SIMILAR PROJECT] for [CLIENT OR CLIENT TYPE], which sounds close to what you're dealing with.

Worth a 20-minute call this week or next? Happy to share how that project went, even if it goes nowhere.

[YOUR NAME]
[LINK TO ONE RELEVANT PIECE OF WORK]
```

**Why this works:** The referrer's name in the subject line borrows their trust before you've earned any. The "even if it goes nowhere" line removes the pressure that makes people ignore sales emails.

---

### 2. The "Heard You're Swamped" Angle

**Send when:** Your contact mentioned the prospect's team is stretched thin, hiring, or behind on something.

**Subject:** [MUTUAL CONTACT] says you have more [WORK TYPE] than hands

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

I was talking to [MUTUAL CONTACT] last week and they mentioned your team is buried in [WORK TYPE] right now.

That's the exact situation I usually get brought into — I handle [SPECIFIC SERVICE] for teams that don't have the bandwidth to do it in-house. Most recently: [ONE-LINE RESULT FOR A SIMILAR CLIENT].

If offloading some of that would help, I can walk you through how I'd slot in. 15 minutes, no deck.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** You're not creating a need, you're responding to one they already voiced to someone else. "No deck" signals you respect their time more than your pitch.

---

### 3. The Shared-Community Opener

**Send when:** You're both in the same Slack group, alumni network, local meetup, or online community — and you can name it honestly.

**Subject:** Fellow [COMMUNITY NAME] member — quick question
**Alt subject:** Saw your post in [COMMUNITY NAME]

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

We're both in [COMMUNITY NAME] — I saw your [POST/COMMENT/TALK] about [TOPIC] and it stuck with me because [ONE GENUINE SENTENCE OF REACTION].

I work with [CLIENT TYPE] on [SERVICE], and [TOPIC] comes up constantly. Curious how you're handling [SPECIFIC RELATED CHALLENGE] at [COMPANY] — and if it's something you'd ever hand to an outside person, I'd love to be in the conversation.

Either way, good to connect with another [COMMUNITY] person.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** Shared context turns a cold email lukewarm, and asking how *they* handle the problem starts a conversation instead of demanding a purchase decision.

---

### B. The Specific-Observation Openers

Use when you noticed something real about their business. The observation is the email — if it's generic, the whole thing collapses.

---

### 4. The "Something's Broken" Note

**Send when:** You found a genuine, fixable issue in their public-facing work (site, checkout, emails, ads) that falls inside your specialty.

**Subject:** Small thing I noticed on [COMPANY]'s [PAGE/FLOW]
**Alt subject:** [COMPANY]'s [SPECIFIC PAGE] — quick heads-up

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

I was on [COMPANY]'s site earlier — [HONEST REASON, e.g. "a friend mentioned your product"] — and noticed [SPECIFIC ISSUE, e.g. "the pricing page takes ~8 seconds to load on mobile"].

I do [SERVICE] for [CLIENT TYPE], so this kind of thing jumps out at me. For [SIMILAR CLIENT], fixing the same issue [CONCRETE OUTCOME, e.g. "lifted trial signups about 15%"].

Want me to send over the two or three other things I spotted? Free, no strings — if you ever want help fixing them, you'll know where I am.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** You've already delivered value before asking for anything, and the offer to send more findings gets a "yes, please" reply — which starts a real thread.

---

### 5. The Content Reaction

**Send when:** They published something — a blog post, podcast episode, LinkedIn post, talk — and you have an actual thought about it.

**Subject:** Your [POST/EPISODE] on [TOPIC]

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

Read your piece on [TOPIC] — the point about [SPECIFIC POINT THEY MADE] matched exactly what I see with clients. [ONE SENTENCE ADDING YOUR OWN OBSERVATION OR DATA POINT.]

It made me wonder how [COMPANY] handles [ADJACENT PROBLEM YOUR SERVICE SOLVES]. That's my corner of the world — I do [SERVICE] for [CLIENT TYPE], most recently [ONE-LINE PROJECT EXAMPLE].

If that's a live question for you, happy to trade notes sometime.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** Creators always read replies to their work. The pivot from their idea to your service is honest and visible — no bait-and-switch, which is exactly why it doesn't feel like one.

---

### 6. The Hiring-Signal Email

**Send when:** They posted a job (or a "we're stretched" signal) in your discipline. Contract help is often faster than the hire they're struggling to make.

**Subject:** Your [ROLE] opening — a stopgap option
**Alt subject:** While you hire that [ROLE]

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

Saw [COMPANY] is hiring a [ROLE]. Those searches usually take a few months, and the work doesn't wait.

I'm a freelance [YOUR ROLE] and I often bridge exactly this gap — carrying the [WORK TYPE] load until the hire lands, then handing off cleanly. Recent example: [ONE-LINE ENGAGEMENT SUMMARY, e.g. "ran lifecycle email for a 20-person SaaS for 5 months between hires"].

If a bridge would take pressure off, I can share how I usually structure it. And if you'd rather wait for the full-timer, completely understood.

[YOUR NAME]
[PORTFOLIO LINK]
```

**Why this works:** The job posting proves budget and need — you're just offering a faster shape of the same solution, and positioning yourself as an ally to the hire, not a threat.

---

### C. The Portfolio-Relevant Openers

Use when your work history is the hook: you've solved their exact problem for someone like them.

---

### 7. The "I Did This Exact Thing" Email

**Send when:** You have a directly comparable project — same industry, same problem, real result you can name.

**Subject:** [RESULT] for a [THEIR INDUSTRY] company like [COMPANY]
**Alt subject:** How [SIMILAR CLIENT TYPE] fixed [PROBLEM]

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

I'll keep this short. Last [TIMEFRAME] I worked with [SIMILAR CLIENT OR "a COMPANY-TYPE like yours"] on [PROBLEM] — we [WHAT YOU DID, ONE CLAUSE], and [CONCRETE RESULT].

I'm reaching out because [COMPANY] looks like it's at a similar stage: [ONE SPECIFIC OBSERVATION ABOUT THEM THAT SUPPORTS THIS].

If [PROBLEM] is on your list this quarter, I'd be glad to walk you through exactly what we did — the parts that worked and the parts that didn't.

[YOUR NAME]
[LINK TO THE CASE STUDY OR WORK SAMPLE]
```

**Why this works:** "The parts that didn't work" is the credibility line — nobody pitching vapor offers to share failures. The specific observation proves this isn't a mail merge.

---

### 8. The Mini-Audit Offer

**Send when:** You can produce a short, genuinely useful review of something they own — and you're willing to do it free as a foot in the door.

**Subject:** 3 quick wins for [COMPANY]'s [ASSET, e.g. onboarding emails]

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

I spent 20 minutes with [COMPANY]'s [ASSET — e.g. "signup flow," "email sequence," "product pages"] this morning. Found three things I'd change:

1. [SPECIFIC, ACTIONABLE OBSERVATION]
2. [SPECIFIC, ACTIONABLE OBSERVATION]
3. [SPECIFIC, ACTIONABLE OBSERVATION — CAN BE A TEASER, e.g. "a checkout issue that's probably costing real money — happy to explain"]

No catch: use these freely. I do [SERVICE] for [CLIENT TYPE], and this is how I introduce myself — by being useful first.

If you'd like the longer list, or a hand implementing any of it, just reply.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** You've done the work most senders only promise to do. Even a "thanks, useful!" reply moves you from stranger to contact — and the teaser in item 3 earns replies on its own.

---

### 9. The Niche-Specialist Flag

**Send when:** You specialize narrowly in their exact type of business and can prove it with a body of work.

**Subject:** I only work with [THEIR NICHE]
**Alt subject:** [SERVICE], but only for [THEIR NICHE]

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

Quick intro: I'm [YOUR NAME], and I do [SERVICE] exclusively for [NICHE — e.g. "independent law firms," "Shopify food brands"]. Not as a marketing line — it's genuinely all I do. Recent work: [CLIENT/PROJECT 1], [CLIENT/PROJECT 2].

Because it's all [NICHE], I already know the standard traps — [TRAP 1] and [TRAP 2] come up in nearly every engagement.

If [PROBLEM AREA] is anywhere on [COMPANY]'s roadmap, I'd welcome the chance to be your shortlist. And if you already have someone good, genuinely — keep them.

[YOUR NAME]
[NICHE-SPECIFIC PORTFOLIO LINK]
```

**Why this works:** Specialists get hired faster and haggled with less. Naming the standard traps proves the specialization is real, and "keep them" reads as confidence, not desperation.

## Section B — The 4-Email Follow-Up Sequence (After No Reply)

The core machine. Most replies to cold outreach come from follow-ups 2–4, not the opener. Spacing from the **original email**: Day 3 → Day 7 → Day 14 → Day 28. Every follow-up must add something new — never send "just bumping this."

---

### 10. Follow-Up 1 — The Useful Nudge (Day 3)

**Send when:** 3 days after your opener, no reply. Reply in the same thread.

**Subject:** (reply in thread — no new subject)

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

Following up on my note from [DAY]. One thing I should have included: [A NEW, USEFUL ITEM — a relevant link, a stat, a one-line insight about their business].

Still happy to [ORIGINAL OFFER, e.g. "walk you through that project"] if useful. If the timing's off, a one-word reply — "later" — works fine and I'll check back in a quarter.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** Adding one new piece of value makes this a second gift, not a second demand. Explicitly permitting "later" gets replies from people who were interested but busy.

---

### 11. Follow-Up 2 — The Different Angle (Day 7)

**Send when:** A week after the opener. Approach the same problem from a new direction — different benefit, different framing, different proof.

**Subject:** (reply in thread) — or new thread: Another thought on [PROBLEM AREA]

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

Different angle on my earlier note. I originally framed this around [ORIGINAL ANGLE, e.g. "saving your team time"], but with [COMPANY-TYPE] businesses the bigger factor is usually [SECOND ANGLE, e.g. "the revenue leaking from PROCESS"].

Case in point: [ONE-SENTENCE EXAMPLE WITH A NUMBER].

Is [PROBLEM] actually a priority right now? If not, no hard feelings — I'd rather know than keep guessing.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** People ignore emails because the first framing didn't land, not because they hate you. A second angle gives the idea a second chance, and asking "is this a priority?" is a question people find easy to answer honestly.

---

### 12. Follow-Up 3 — The Proof Drop (Day 14)

**Send when:** Two weeks in. Lead with evidence: a case study, result, or named client story.

**Subject:** How [SIMILAR CLIENT] handled [PROBLEM]

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

Thought this might be more useful than another check-in: a short write-up of how [SIMILAR CLIENT] dealt with [PROBLEM].

The short version — [ONE-SENTENCE SITUATION], we [WHAT YOU DID], and within [TIMEFRAME] they saw [CONCRETE RESULT].

Full story here if you want it: [LINK]

If [COMPANY] is wrestling with anything similar, I'm around.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** By the third touch, claims are noise — evidence is signal. Framing it as "more useful than another check-in" acknowledges the follow-up dance with a wink instead of pretending it isn't happening.

---

### 13. Follow-Up 4 — The Easy Out (Day 28)

**Send when:** A month in, still nothing. Last touch of the sequence — make "no" effortless.

**Subject:** (reply in thread) — or: Closing the loop on [TOPIC]

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

Last note from me on this. I'll assume [PROBLEM AREA] isn't a priority right now — which is a completely fine answer.

Two things before I go quiet:

1. If it becomes a priority later, this thread will still work.
2. If I've been emailing the wrong person, I'd appreciate a point in the right direction.

Thanks for the inbox space either way.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** Removing all pressure often triggers the reply the pressure was blocking. The "wrong person" ask gives them a face-saving way to respond without committing to anything.

---

### 14. The One-Line Bump (Swap-In)

**Send when:** As an alternate Follow-Up 1 — only when your opener was strong and specific enough to carry a second read on its own.

**Subject:** (reply in thread)

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME] — floating this back up in case it got buried. The short version: [ONE-SENTENCE RESTATEMENT OF THE OFFER]. Worth a look?

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** Sometimes the only problem was inbox position. Two lines respects that possibility without piling on — but it only works when the original email did the heavy lifting.

---

### 15. The "Wrong Person?" Redirect (Swap-In)

**Send when:** As an alternate Follow-Up 2 or 3 — especially at larger companies where you may genuinely have guessed wrong.

**Subject:** (reply in thread) — or: Who owns [AREA] at [COMPANY]?

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

Quick process question rather than another pitch: are you the right person for [AREA, e.g. "website decisions"] at [COMPANY], or does that sit with someone else?

If it's someone else, I'd be grateful for a name — I'll take it from there and stop cluttering your inbox.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** It's a genuinely easy question, and forwarded intros ("talk to Dana") convert far better than cold openers because they arrive pre-endorsed by a colleague.

## Section C — Post-Proposal Follow-Ups

You sent the proposal; now the silence. Suggested spacing: Day 2 → Day 6 → Day 11 → Day 16 → Day 21 → Day 30. **Use three or four of these six**, chosen for your situation — not all of them.

---

### 16. The Receipt Check (Day 2)

**Send when:** Two days after sending the proposal, no acknowledgment.

**Subject:** (reply in proposal thread)

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

Wanted to make sure the proposal landed — attachments and links sometimes get eaten.

No rush on a decision. But if anything in there is unclear or raises questions, I'd rather untangle it now than have it stall things silently. Happy to hop on a 10-minute call or just answer by email.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** "Did it arrive?" is pressure-free and always answerable. Naming the real risk — silent stalling over an unasked question — invites the objection out into the open early.

---

### 17. The Silent-Objection Opener (Day 5–7)

**Send when:** They confirmed receipt (or you know they read it) but have gone quiet. Price is usually the unspoken issue.

**Subject:** The honest question about the proposal

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

I'll ask directly, because guessing helps neither of us: is anything in the proposal giving you pause — price, scope, timeline?

If it's budget, say so plainly. There's often a smaller way to start — [EXAMPLE, e.g. "phase one only, or a narrower scope on X"] — and I'd rather reshape the plan than lose the fit over a number.

And if the honest answer is "we're going another way," that's useful too. I'll close the file gracefully, promise.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** Silence after a proposal almost always hides an objection nobody wants to voice. Naming price yourself makes it discussable, and pre-forgiving a "no" makes an honest reply feel safe.

---

### 18. The Real Deadline (Day 10–12)

**Send when:** Only when you genuinely have limited capacity or a start-date constraint. Never fabricate this.

**Subject:** Scheduling note on the [PROJECT NAME] proposal

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

A practical heads-up rather than a nudge: I'm currently planning [MONTH]'s schedule, and I have [HONEST CAPACITY, e.g. "one project slot"] left that would fit [PROJECT NAME].

If you're leaning yes, a quick word this week lets me hold it. If you need longer, that's fine too — it just means the start date moves to [REALISTIC LATER DATE] or so.

Whatever's true on your end, I'd love a status check, even a one-liner.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** Real scarcity, plainly stated, prompts decisions without the sleaze of fake countdown timers. The fallback ("start date moves") shows you're informing them, not cornering them.

---

### 19. The Smaller Door (Day 15+)

**Send when:** The full proposal seems to have stalled on size. Offer a scoped-down way to start.

**Subject:** A smaller way to start [PROJECT NAME]

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

Since the full [PROJECT NAME] plan may be more than you want to commit to right now, here's a smaller door:

[SCOPED-DOWN OPTION — e.g. "a 2-week discovery sprint covering X and Y"] for [PRICE]. Standalone value on its own, and if we continue afterward, it counts toward the full project.

Same quality, smaller bet. Want me to send a one-page version of that?

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** Big proposals stall because big decisions are scary. A cheap, self-contained first step converts "we need to think" into "sure, let's try that" — and small starts routinely grow into full engagements.

---

### 20. The Internal-Pitch Assist (Anytime They Have a Boss)

**Send when:** Your contact likes the proposal but needs sign-off from a partner, boss, or committee.

**Subject:** Making the internal case for [PROJECT NAME]

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

Since this needs a green light from [BOSS/PARTNER/THE TEAM], want me to make that conversation easier?

I can put together a half-page summary — the problem, the plan, cost, expected return, timeline — written for someone who hasn't read the full proposal. You forward it, or crib from it, whatever's useful.

Anything specific [DECISION-MAKER] will push on? Tell me and I'll address it head-on.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** Deals die in meetings you're not in. Arming your champion with a forwardable, skimmable case dramatically raises the odds — and asking what the decision-maker will push on surfaces the real objection.

---

### 21. The Proposal Sunset (Day 30)

**Send when:** A month of silence. Close the proposal cleanly while leaving the door open.

**Subject:** Wrapping up the [PROJECT NAME] proposal

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

It's been about a month, so I'm going to mark the [PROJECT NAME] proposal as closed and release the pricing and scheduling in it — I don't want an expired document floating around as if it's current.

If things change, reach out anytime and I'll put together fresh numbers. And if you went another direction, no explanation needed — thanks for considering the work at all.

Good luck with [PROJECT/COMPANY GOAL] either way.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** Formally expiring the proposal creates a real (not manufactured) decision point, protects you from someone accepting stale pricing in six months, and leaves the last impression as pure class.

## Section D — Reviving Ghosted Clients

For prospects or clients who were actively talking to you — then vanished mid-conversation. Different from cold follow-up: there was a relationship. Start about a week into the silence.

---

### 22. The No-Guilt Check-In

**Send when:** About a week after they went quiet mid-discussion. First revival touch.

**Subject:** (reply in existing thread)

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

Picking our thread back up — we were talking about [WHERE THINGS LEFT OFF] and then, as happens, life intervened.

No guilt implied; my inbox does the same thing to people. Just tell me which is true:

a) still interested, timing slipped
b) this fell down the priority list
c) it's a no for now

Any single letter works as a full reply.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** Ghosting is usually embarrassment plus busyness, not rejection. Removing the guilt and shrinking the reply cost to one keystroke clears both obstacles at once.

---

### 23. The "Priorities Changed?" Direct Ask

**Send when:** Second revival touch, about a week after #22. Slightly more direct.

**Subject:** Did [PROJECT/TOPIC] fall off the list?

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

When we last spoke, [PROJECT/TOPIC] sounded like a real priority — you mentioned [SPECIFIC THING THEY SAID, e.g. "wanting it live before the spring launch"].

Has that changed? Genuine question, not a guilt trip. Priorities shift and plans die; it happens to half the projects I ever discuss.

If it's still on, I'm ready to pick up exactly where we left off. If it's off, telling me takes ten seconds and I'll stop nudging.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** Quoting their own words is respectful proof you were listening — and it gently re-invokes the motivation they had when they said it. The explicit "it happens" makes an honest "it's off" easy to send.

---

### 24. The New-Info Revival

**Send when:** You have something genuinely new to share — a relevant article, a competitor move, a new result of yours, a tool change that affects their plan.

**Subject:** Saw this and thought of [COMPANY/PROJECT]

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

Something crossed my desk that's relevant to the [PROJECT/TOPIC] conversation we were having: [THE NEW THING — one sentence, with link if applicable].

The reason it matters for you: [ONE SENTENCE CONNECTING IT TO THEIR SITUATION].

It nudged our conversation back to mind. Any appetite for picking that up again?

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** This reads as thoughtfulness, not chasing — you contacted them because something reminded you of them, which is true. It restarts the conversation on fresh fuel instead of reheated ask.

---

### 25. The Mid-Project Unblock

**Send when:** An active client has gone quiet while you're waiting on their feedback, assets, or approval — and the project is stalling.

**Subject:** [PROJECT NAME] is paused on my end — here's what unblocks it

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

Status check on [PROJECT NAME]: I'm currently blocked waiting on [SPECIFIC THING — e.g. "your feedback on the v2 designs" / "the product photos"]. Once I have it, I need about [TIMEFRAME] to finish [NEXT MILESTONE].

I know these things land on a pile. If it helps, we can do a 15-minute call and I'll take notes instead of you writing anything. Or if [THING] is stuck for a reason I should know about, tell me and we'll re-plan around it.

One heads-up so it's not a surprise later: if the pause runs past [DATE], I'll need to shift the remaining work to my next open slot, which would move delivery to around [NEW DATE].

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** It converts a vague guilt-nudge into a clear operational picture: what's blocked, what unblocks it, what delay costs. The call offer removes their real obstacle (writing feedback is work), and the schedule note creates honest consequences.

---

### 26. The Gentle File-Close

**Send when:** Two or three revival attempts over ~3 weeks have gone unanswered. Final touch before the archive.

**Subject:** Closing the file on [PROJECT/TOPIC] (easy to reopen)

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

I've checked in a few times without luck, so I'll take the hint gracefully and close the file on [PROJECT/TOPIC].

Nothing dramatic — no hard feelings, and reopening it costs one email whenever you're ready. I've saved [WHAT YOU'LL KEEP — notes, drafts, scope work] so we wouldn't start from zero.

Wishing you a smooth [SEASON/QUARTER], and hope our paths cross again.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** "Closing the file" is a soft deadline that converts lurkers without threatening anyone, and mentioning the saved work reminds them there's real value sitting one reply away.

## Section E — Past-Client Re-Engagement

Your warmest list: people who already paid you and liked it. One touch per quarter, rotating these five so it never feels like a campaign.

---

### 27. The Results Check-In

**Send when:** 2–3 months after a project wrapped. Pure follow-through, with a soft door at the end.

**Subject:** How's [THE THING YOU BUILT/DELIVERED] holding up?

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

It's been about [TIMEFRAME] since we wrapped [PROJECT], and I genuinely wonder how it's performing. Are you seeing movement on [THE METRIC OR OUTCOME THE PROJECT TARGETED]?

Two reasons I ask: I like knowing my work actually worked, and if anything's underperforming, small post-launch adjustments usually fix more than people expect.

Either way, I'd love the update — even a two-line one.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** Almost nobody follows up on their own delivered work — doing it puts you in the top percentile of vendors instantly. "Underperforming" opens the door to paid follow-on work without a pitch in sight.

---

### 28. The Spotted Opportunity

**Send when:** You noticed something on a past client's site/marketing/product — an issue or an opportunity — while going about your business.

**Subject:** Noticed something on [COMPANY]'s [ASSET]

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

I was on [WHERE YOU SAW IT] today and spotted something worth flagging: [THE SPECIFIC OBSERVATION — e.g. "the new landing page doesn't link to the case studies we built" / "your checkout throws an error on mobile Safari"].

[ONE SENTENCE ON WHY IT MATTERS — money, trust, or traffic.]

Might be intentional! But if it's not, it's probably a quick fix — [ROUGH SIZE, e.g. "an hour or two"]. Want me to handle it, or just wanted you to know it's there.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** You're doing free quality-assurance on work you care about — the strongest retention behavior there is. "Might be intentional!" keeps it collegial instead of critical.

---

### 29. The New-Service Note

**Send when:** You've added a service, and this specific client is a genuine fit for it.

**Subject:** New from my desk — relevant to [COMPANY], I think

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

Short note: I've started offering [NEW SERVICE], and you're one of a handful of past clients I thought of specifically — because of [SPECIFIC REASON TIED TO THEIR BUSINESS, e.g. "how much of your traffic was mobile when we ran the audit"].

The shape of it: [ONE OR TWO SENTENCES — what it is, what it costs or how it's scoped].

Since we've worked together, I'm offering it to past clients first, at [PAST-CLIENT TERMS — early rate, first pick of slots, whatever's true]. Interested in the details?

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** "I thought of you specifically, and here's the evidence" separates this from a broadcast. Past-client-first terms reward loyalty and create honest, deadline-free preference.

---

### 30. The Planning-Season Nudge

**Send when:** 4–8 weeks before their natural planning or peak season — new year, Q4, back-to-school, their industry's big month.

**Subject:** [SEASON/EVENT] prep — worth a conversation?

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

[SEASON/EVENT] is about [X WEEKS] out, which in my calendar means: everyone calls in [MONTH BEFORE], and the people who called earlier get the good timelines.

Last [SAME SEASON], [RELEVANT REFERENCE — e.g. "we scrambled to get the campaign pages live in time" / "you mentioned wanting to redo X before the rush"]. If there's a version of that this year, now is the cheap-and-calm time to plan it.

Want to grab 20 minutes in the next couple of weeks and see if anything's worth doing?

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** You're selling calm instead of services — booking early is genuinely in their interest, so the nudge is a favor. Referencing last season's scramble is memory-jogging proof this isn't hypothetical.

---

### 31. The Capacity Note

**Send when:** You have an actual opening coming up and want your best past clients to get first claim. Send to a handful of people, individually.

**Subject:** An opening in my [MONTH] schedule

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

Quick heads-up before I fill it: a project wraps at the end of [MONTH], which leaves me an open slot from [DATE].

You're one of the first people I'm telling, partly because working with [COMPANY] was a genuine pleasure, and partly because you mentioned [THING THEY ONCE SAID THEY WANTED — e.g. "eventually redoing the reporting dashboards"]. If "eventually" has gotten closer, the timing is unusually good.

No pressure whatsoever — if nothing's brewing, ignore this freely. The slot will find a home.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** Real scarcity plus real memory ("you mentioned...") makes this flattering rather than needy. "The slot will find a home" keeps your status high — you're offering first pick, not begging for work.

---

## Section F — Invoice Reminders (Friendly → Firm)

Money emails. Calm, factual, unembarrassed — you did the work; asking to be paid is not rude. Rhythm: heads-up at day −3, then day +2, +9, +18 past due. Attach or link the invoice **every single time.**

---

### 32. The Pre-Due Heads-Up (3 Days Before)

**Send when:** Three days before the due date — especially with new clients or bigger invoices. Prevents half of all late payments.

**Subject:** Invoice [NUMBER] — due [DATE]

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

Friendly calendar note: invoice [NUMBER] ([AMOUNT], for [WORK DESCRIPTION]) is due this [DAY, DATE].

Everything you need is attached, and the payment link is here: [LINK]. If it's already moving through your process — great, ignore me.

Thanks as always,
[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** Most "late" payments are just unprocessed ones. A pre-due nudge catches the invoice while it's findable, with zero awkwardness because nothing is late yet.

---

### 33. Stage 1 — The Friendly Nudge (2–3 Days Past Due)

**Send when:** A couple of days past due. Assume good faith completely.

**Subject:** Invoice [NUMBER] — quick nudge

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

Invoice [NUMBER] ([AMOUNT]) came due on [DATE] and hasn't landed yet — most likely it just slipped through a crack somewhere, as invoices do.

I've re-attached it, and here's the payment link again: [LINK].

If it's already been sent, ignore this with my blessing. And if something's holding it up, just tell me — always easier to work with than silence.

Thanks!
[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** Blaming "a crack somewhere" instead of a person lets everyone save face. The "tell me what's holding it up" line opens a channel for the client with a real problem — which you want to know about now, not at day 30.

---

### 34. Stage 2 — Firm but Warm (9–10 Days Past Due)

**Send when:** Over a week past due, first nudge unanswered. Warmth stays; vagueness goes.

**Subject:** Invoice [NUMBER] — now [X] days overdue

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

Following up again on invoice [NUMBER] for [AMOUNT], now [X] days past its [DATE] due date. Invoice re-attached; payment link: [LINK].

I need one of two things by [SPECIFIC DATE, ~3–4 DAYS OUT]:

1. Payment, or
2. A reply telling me when payment will arrive and why it's delayed.

If cash flow is the issue, say so — I'd rather set up a split payment than keep sending reminders. But I do need a response this time; silence is the one option that doesn't work.

Thanks,
[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** Two acceptable options and a specific date convert a vague hope into a clear next step. Offering a payment plan proves the firmness is about respect, not aggression — while "silence doesn't work" ends the ambiguity.

---

### 35. Stage 3 — The Formal Notice (18–21 Days Past Due)

**Send when:** ~3 weeks overdue, previous reminders ignored. State consequences you're actually prepared to follow through on.

**Subject:** Overdue invoice [NUMBER] — action required by [DATE]

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

Invoice [NUMBER] for [AMOUNT] is now [X] days overdue, and my reminders on [DATE 1] and [DATE 2] have gone unanswered.

I want to resolve this simply, so here's where things stand. If payment (or a concrete payment plan) isn't in place by [DATE, ~5–7 DAYS OUT]:

- [CONSEQUENCE PER YOUR CONTRACT — e.g. "the 1.5% monthly late fee in our agreement takes effect"]
- [IF APPLICABLE — "work on PROJECT pauses until the account is current"]
- [IF TRUE — "I'll hand the invoice to my collections process, which I'd honestly rather not do"]

None of this is the outcome I want. One reply — even "we're struggling, can we talk?" — takes all of it off the table.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** Documented history plus specific consequences plus a specific date is the structure that gets stalled invoices paid. The final line keeps the human door open right up to the end — which is also exactly how you'd want a court or collections agency to see you behaved.

---

### 36. The Broken-Promise Follow-Up

**Send when:** They told you payment was coming ("this Friday!") and the date passed. Slots in anywhere in the sequence.

**Subject:** Invoice [NUMBER] — following up on [DAY THEY PROMISED]

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

On [DATE], you mentioned invoice [NUMBER] ([AMOUNT]) would be paid by [PROMISED DATE]. That's come and gone and it hasn't arrived, so I wanted to flag it in case something failed silently — payments do misfire.

Could you check on your end and tell me today where it stands? If it went out, a confirmation or reference number sorts everything. If it didn't, I need a new date I can actually rely on.

Thanks,
[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** Quoting their own commitment makes the follow-up unarguable without being aggressive. "A date I can actually rely on" names the broken promise honestly but leaves them room to fix it with dignity.

---

## Section G — Asking for Referrals & Testimonials

The highest-ROI emails a freelancer can send, and the least sent. The moment matters more than the words: ask within 72 hours of a win, while enthusiasm is real.

---

### 37. The Hot-Moment Testimonial Ask

**Send when:** Within a day or two of a happy moment — launch day, a great result, an email from them saying "this is amazing."

**Subject:** A favor while [PROJECT] is fresh

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

So glad [PROJECT] landed the way it did — [ONE SPECIFIC CALLBACK, e.g. "seeing the signup numbers from launch week was genuinely fun"].

While it's fresh: would you write a few sentences I can use as a testimonial? Nothing formal — the useful version answers some of:

- What was the situation before?
- What changed?
- What would you tell someone considering working with me?

Three or four honest sentences beat a polished paragraph every time. And thank you — this stuff genuinely keeps my business running.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** Enthusiasm decays fast — the same ask converts several times better in week one than in month two. The guiding questions rescue clients from blank-page paralysis, which is the #1 reason testimonial asks go unanswered.

---

### 38. The Pre-Drafted Testimonial

**Send when:** Your client is thrilled but slammed — or has already ignored one testimonial ask. You do the writing; they do the approving.

**Subject:** Testimonial — I did the hard part

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

I know writing a testimonial is one more task on a long list, so I drafted one from things you've actually said to me during the project:

"[2–3 SENTENCE DRAFT BUILT FROM THEIR REAL WORDS AND REAL RESULTS — e.g. 'We'd been putting off the rebuild for two years because every agency quoted us six months. NAME delivered in eight weeks and the new site converts nearly twice as well. Straightforward to work with from kickoff to launch.']"

Edit anything, rewrite it entirely, or reply "approved" and I'll use it as is — with your name and [COMPANY], if that's okay.

If it doesn't reflect how you actually feel, toss it and tell me — honesty outranks marketing here.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** You've reduced their effort to one word. Building the draft from their real quotes keeps it truthful, and the permission to toss it protects the trust that makes any of this worth having.

---

### 39. The Review-Platform Ask

**Send when:** You need public reviews where prospects actually look — Google, Clutch, Upwork, an industry directory. Send after (or instead of) a private testimonial.

**Subject:** One small ask — 3 minutes, big impact

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

One small ask, then I'll get out of your inbox: would you leave a short review on [PLATFORM]? Direct link, no hunting required: [LINK]

For a business like mine, [PLATFORM] reviews are roughly what word-of-mouth used to be — a few honest sentences from a real client outweigh anything I could say about myself.

If you've only got one review in you this year, I'd love it to be this one. And thanks — truly.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** The direct link removes the friction where most review intentions die, and "3 minutes" sets an honest, small expectation. The light touch keeps it feeling like a favor between people, not a corporate NPS blast.

---

### 40. The Project-Close Referral Ask

**Send when:** In your wrap-up email or right after it, while the finished work is the freshest thing in their mind.

**Subject:** Before we close the file — one question

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

With [PROJECT] wrapped (and wrapped well, I hope), one question I ask every client I've enjoyed working with:

Who else do you know that's wrestling with [THE PROBLEM YOU SOLVE]? Often it's [WHERE THEIR PEERS ARE — e.g. "another founder in your network," "a peer from your industry group"].

If someone comes to mind, an intro email with both of us on it is perfect — I'll take it from there and I promise to make you look good for the referral.

And regardless: thank you for [PROJECT]. It was a genuinely good one.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** "Who do you know that..." with a named problem beats a vague "any referrals?" because it triggers an actual memory search. "I'll make you look good" answers the referrer's private worry — that recommending you is a risk to their own reputation.

---

### 41. The Past-Client Referral Ask

**Send when:** Reaching back to happy past clients when you're building pipeline. Works best with a specific description of who you're looking for.

**Subject:** Do you know anyone like this?

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

Hope [COMPANY] is thriving — I still point people to [PROJECT YOU DID FOR THEM] as some of my favorite work.

A direct ask: I've got room for [NUMBER] new clients this [QUARTER/SEASON], and my best work is with [SPECIFIC PROFILE — e.g. "B2B SaaS teams of 10–50 that are outgrowing their founder-built website"].

Does anyone in your world fit that? A simple intro — "you two should talk" — is all it takes, and whether they hire me or not, I'll make sure the conversation is worth their time.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** A specific profile does the client's thinking for them — vague asks get vague silence. Naming limited capacity frames you as selective, and the "worth their time either way" promise de-risks the intro.

---

### 42. The Case-Study Ask

**Send when:** A project produced results worth documenting, a month or three after wrap when the numbers have matured.

**Subject:** [PROJECT]'s numbers are worth writing up — may I?

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

The results from [PROJECT] have had time to settle, and they're good — [THE HEADLINE NUMBER OR OUTCOME]. I'd love to write it up as a proper case study.

What that means for you: about 20 minutes on a call for a couple of quotes, then you approve the final draft before anything goes public. Numbers can be exact or anonymized ("a [INDUSTRY] company") — your call entirely.

What's in it for you: a polished write-up of your win, free to reuse in your own marketing, board decks, wherever.

Game?

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** Draft approval and anonymization options dissolve the two real objections before they're raised. Framing the case study as a reusable asset for *them* turns a favor into a trade.

---

### 43. The Referral Thank-You

**Send when:** Immediately when a referral comes in — before you even know if it becomes a project.

**Subject:** [REFERRED PERSON] reached out — thank you

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

[REFERRED PERSON] just got in touch, and they mentioned you sent them my way. Thank you — a referral from someone who's actually worked with me is worth more than any marketing I could buy.

I'll take excellent care of them, whether or not it turns into a project. And your judgment is clearly good, so: if there's ever anything I can do for you — a quick question answered, a look at something, an intro from my side — the answer is yes.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** Thanking at the intro (not the invoice) rewards the behavior you want more of. "Whether or not it turns into a project" reassures them their contact will be treated well — which is what determines whether they ever refer again.

---

## Section H — Breakup Emails That Get Replies

The paradox email: announcing you'll stop emailing generates more replies than any follow-up before it. Rules: send exactly one, mean it, and keep all bitterness out — the tone is "closing a browser tab," not "storming out."

---

### 44. The Classic Permission-to-Close

**Send when:** Your standard end-of-sequence breakup — after the Section B or C sequence has run dry. The default choice.

**Subject:** Should I close your file?

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

I've reached out a few times about [TOPIC] without luck, and I don't want to become inbox noise.

So, last email: unless I hear otherwise, I'll close your file and stop following up. No hard feelings — timing, budgets, and priorities are real, and silence usually just means "not now."

If "not now" ever becomes "now," this thread will still be here, and so will I.

All the best with [COMPANY GOAL OR PROJECT AREA],
[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** Loss aversion is real — an open door quietly closing prompts action where open doors never did. The graceful exit costs nothing and is remembered; a meaningful share of breakup emails get replies within hours.

---

### 45. The Multiple-Choice Breakup

**Send when:** You want the breakup to do double duty as a diagnostic — learning *why* they went quiet.

**Subject:** One letter and I'm gone

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

Final follow-up on [TOPIC] — and to make it painless, it's multiple choice. Which is closest?

a) Interested, but buried — try me in a month
b) Not a priority this [QUARTER/YEAR] — try me in [SEASON]
c) We went with someone else / solved it internally
d) Please stop emailing me

Reply with a single letter and I'll do exactly what it says. No letter, and I'll assume (d) and bow out gracefully.

Thanks for the inbox space either way.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** A one-keystroke reply is the lowest-friction ask in email, and even a (c) is a gift — it tells you the lead is dead so you stop spending attention on it. The (a) and (b) replies hand you scheduled, pre-authorized re-engagement.

---

### 46. The Light-Touch Breakup

**Send when:** The prospect's vibe is informal and your earlier emails had some personality. Charm, not shtick.

**Subject:** My last email (I promise)

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

This is my last email about [TOPIC] — I promise, and unlike most people who say that in cold email, I'll actually keep it.

I figure one of three things happened: it's not a fit, it's not the moment, or my emails have been arriving during the exact minute you look at your inbox each day and losing to actual emergencies. All three are fair.

If it's ever useful, here's the one link worth keeping: [YOUR SINGLE BEST RESOURCE — case study, portfolio, guide]. And if we never talk again, sincerely — good luck with [THEIR GOAL/PROJECT AREA].

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** Gentle self-awareness is disarming precisely because most sales email has none. The parting resource means even the goodbye delivers value — which is what they'll remember if the need resurfaces.

---

### 47. The Proposal Breakup

**Send when:** Specifically after a *proposal* stalled — Section C ran its course and template 21's sunset got no reply either. Sharper than a cold-lead breakup because real work went in.

**Subject:** Releasing the [PROJECT NAME] slot

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

Since I haven't been able to get a read on the [PROJECT NAME] proposal, I'm going to call it: I'll release the scheduling I was holding and mark this one closed on my end.

For whatever it's worth, the thinking in that proposal — [ONE GENUINELY USEFUL IDEA FROM IT] — is yours to use regardless of who does the work. I'd rather it help you than sit in a dead PDF.

If the project resurfaces down the road, I'd still be glad to talk. It would just start with fresh scoping and current pricing.

Good luck with it, sincerely.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** Gifting an idea from the proposal is the classiest possible exit — it proves the value was real and stings exactly enough to prompt "wait, let's talk" from prospects who were still deciding. The fresh-pricing note protects you either way.

---

### 48. The Dormant-List Goodbye

**Send when:** Doing an annual cleanup of leads and past contacts who've been dark for a year or more. Send individually, not as a blast.

**Subject:** Cleaning out my files — you're in them

```text
Hi [FIRST NAME],

Once a year I go through my contact list and close the loops I left open, and I found ours — we last talked in [MONTH/YEAR] about [TOPIC].

If things have changed and [PROBLEM AREA] is back on the menu, I'd genuinely love to hear where things stand — a lot has changed on my end too ([ONE-LINE UPDATE — new service, new results, new focus]).

If not, no reply needed. Consider the loop gracefully closed, and consider this a standing invitation for whenever it's ever useful.

[YOUR NAME]
```

**Why this works:** "Annual cleanup" is an honest, unembarrassing reason to reappear after a year of silence. It costs you nothing, revives a surprising number of dead threads, and the standing invitation ends every relationship on warm terms.

## Appendix — The Rules of the Machine

1. **Follow-ups win.** Across every sequence in this library, most replies come from touches 2–4. Sending only openers is leaving most of your pipeline on the table.
2. **Every touch adds something.** New info, new angle, new proof, or a new (easier) way to say no. "Just bumping this" adds nothing and teaches people to ignore you.
3. **Spacing widens over time.** Day 3, then 7, then 14, then 28. Early speed shows attentiveness; later patience shows respect.
4. **One ask per email.** A question that can be answered in one line, from a phone, in an elevator.
5. **Real reasons only.** Real deadlines, real capacity, real observations. The moment you fake urgency, everything else in the email becomes suspect too.
6. **The breakup is one email.** Send it, mean it, stop. The credibility of every future email you ever send depends on it.
